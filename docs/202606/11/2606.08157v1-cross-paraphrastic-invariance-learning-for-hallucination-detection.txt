ABSTRACT 

Large language models (LLMs) frequently generate hallucinations ,which are unsupported by a source document. To avoid costly LLM-as-evaluator pipelines and the heavy annotation demands of existing classifiers, we propose CPIL (Cross Paraphrastic Invari-ance Learning ), a two-stage Siamese framework that maximizes the utility of existing labeled data. Concretely, CPIL constructs infor-mative training pairs by: (i) generating paraphrastic views of each document-claim example as positives, and explicitly aligning their representations to enforce invariance to surface form; and (ii) mining 

same-document, opposite-label pairs as hard negatives to sharpen document-sensitive decision boundaries. Then CPIL conduct a two-stage model training: Stage 1 performs contrastive pretraining to learn a paraphrase-invariant, grounding-aware embedding space; and Stage 2 attaches a lightweight classifier for binary grounded-ness. On the LLM-AggreFact benchmark (11 tasks), CPIL surpasses strong baselines concerning F1 scores with only ∼ 1% labeled data, showing its prediction superiority and label efficiency .

Index Terms — Hallucination detection, contrastive learning, siamese neural network 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) show impressive fluency and coher-ence across a wide range of tasks. However, a persistent challenge is hallucinations , the generation of statements that are factually in-correct or unsupported by a given source document [1, 2]. Accu-rately detecting hallucination is essential for developing trustworthy, safety-aligned AI systems [3]. Prior work on hallucination detection broadly falls into two fam-ilies [1]. (i) LLM-as-evaluator methods (e.g., SelfCheckGPT [4], SelfCheckAgent [5], and G-Eval [6]) query powerful LLMs to cri-tique or verify generated content. However, they are often expen-sive at inference time and tokens, as a single instance may require multiple LLM prompts (e.g., iterative entity-level checks or chain-of-thought reasoning [7]). (ii) Factuality classifiers train compact models to predict whether a claim is grounded in a source document, and are typically much more efficient to deploy. For example, Sum-maC [8] adapts pre-trained natural language inference (NLI) models for document-summary consistency; QAFactEval [9] probes docu-ments by question answering; AlignScore [10] aggregates NLI and factuality supervision; MiniCheck [11] uses LLMs to label large collections of document-claim pairs; and FactCG [12] synthesizes multi-hop claims via graph-based augmentation. We propose CPIL (Cross Paraphrastic Invariance Learning ), a two-stage Siamese framework for document-grounded hallucina-tion detection that better exploit existing labeled data without ad-ditional human or LLM annotation. The key is to construct infor-mative positive/negative training pairs from a few (e.g., 100) la-beled samples and adopt a Siamese Network [13, 14] to leverage far more supervision signal than a standard classification approach. Concretely, we use back-translation [15] to produce paraphrastic variants of each document-claim instance. Instead of simply ap-pending paraphrases to the training data [16], CPIL explicitly aligns the representations of the same instance’s cross-paraphrases (as pos-itives), encouraging invariance to lexical and syntactic variations. For negatives, rather than arbitrary cross-document mismatches [17], CPIL samples same-document, opposite-label pairs, yielding harder contrasts. After forming the pairing set tailored to hallucination detection, CPIL instantiates Siamese networks whose training pro-ceeds in two stages: Stage 1 performs contrastive pretraining to learn a paraphrase-invariant embedding space by (i) pulling together cross-paraphrase views of the same instance and (ii) pushing apart pairs that share the document but differ in label. Stage 2 attaches a lightweight classifier and optimizes a binary groundedness with limited labeled data. Empirically, CPIL outperforms strong baselines, including FactCG [12], MiniCheck [11], AlignScore [10], and SummaC [8], in average F1 score on LLM-AggreFact benchmark [11]. Moreover, CPIL is markedly label-efficient : it maintains strong performance with only a small fraction of supervised (e.g., 1% of labels), surpass-ing baselines trained on the full (or larger) datasets. Ablation studies corroborate the importance of our pairing and training strategies.

2. PROPOSED METHOD 

We begin by formalizing the task and notations in Sec. 2.1. We then introduce CPIL, our two-stage Siamese framework illustrated in Fig. 2. Sec. 2.2 describes how we obtain label-preserving paraphras-tic views and construct informative training pairs. Sec. 2.3 details contrastive pretraining and the classifier fine-tuning stages. 

2.1. Problem Setup 

Document-grounded hallucination detection is framed as a binary classification task [18]. Let training set be D = {(di, c i, y i)}Ni=1 ,where di is a document, ci is a claim about di, and yi ∈ { 0, 1}

indicates whether the claim is supported (1) or hallucinated (0). The goal is to learn a detector h(d, c ) = Pr( y = 1 | d, c ).

Rather than collecting additional annotations [12], CPIL max-imizes the utility of the existing labeled subset Ds ⊂ D by con-structing high-quality pairings. A na¨ ıve pairing strategy [17] forms positives from two randomly sampled examples that share a label and negatives otherwise. However, it may not suit to hallucination detection: examples drawn from different sources can be topically and lexically dissimilar, yielding weak alignment signals. Thus, CPIL creates dense and informative supervision pairings by cross-paraphrase positives and same-document hard negatives .

3. EXPERIMENTS 3.1. Experiment Settings Evaluation & metrics. We evaluate on LLM-AggreFact , an aggre-gate benchmark comprising 11 factuality datasets [11]. We report per-task F1 (%) and the macro-averaged F1 (AVG) across all tasks. We follow the official splits and evaluation protocols. 

Baselines. CPIL is compared against four powerful non-LLM detectors: SummaC (ZS and CV variants) [8], AlignScore [10], 

MiniCheck [11], and FactCG [12]. When available, we use authors’ released implementations and recommended checkpoints. 

Implementation details. For CPIL, the encoder fθ is instan-tiated with DeBERTa [23], and the classifier gϕ is a single-layer MLP. Paraphrastic views are generated via back-translation using three pivots, P = {fr , es , zh } (French, Spanish, Chinese). Un-less noted otherwise, optimization settings follow FactCG [12], and we use a learning rate of 1×10 −5.

Data Budget and Pair Generation. Starting from the dataset 

D released with FactCG [12] (approximately 16 .7k labeled sam-ples), our three-pivot paraphrasing and pairing procedure can yield up to more than 350k training pairs. To study label efficiency under capped supervision, Ds only uses ∼ 2% labeled data in D, yield-ing about 7k training pairs; and a lower budget variant using about 

∼ 1% labels leads to about 4k pairs. We denote these configurations as CPIL-2% and CPIL-1% , respectively. 

3.2. Main Results 

As reported in Table 1, CPIL attains the best macro average (79.14 with 1% labels) and the second-best (78.64 with 2% labels), outper-forming all baselines on average. The largest margins are from CNN 

and MeetB , where paraphrase invariance and document-sensitive contrasts are especially beneficial given style variability and multi-speaker noise. Tasks such as WiCE and RAGTruth, which are sensitive to word sense and entity fidelity, remain challenging, while CPIL stays competitive. Both the 1% and 2% configurations surpass all baselines trained at full or larger data scale. Thus, CPIL converts limited labels into richer supervision via pairwise alignment, yielding higher accuracy without additional annotation and inference overhead.

3.4. Ablation Study 

We ablate CPIL along two axes: (i) data utilization (pairing/training strategy) and (ii) pivot selection (paraphraser). All ablations con-sume comparable supervision budgets by downsampling to about 

16k training pairs/samples, and results are summarized in Table 2. 

(i) Data utilization. We consider three variants that use the same labeled pool D but differ in how examples are consumed: (a) 

ClsOnly : omits contrastive pretraining (Stage 1), and trains a clas-sifier on original D only. (b) Append : also skips Stage 1, but di-rectly appends paraphrases to D without explicit view alignment. (c) RandPair : forms positives/negatives by randomly pairing same-/opposite-label instances, irrespective of document identity. 

Pair construction matters. Both Append and RandPair out-perform ClsOnly, indicating that additional supervision signals, whether via paraphrastic augmentation or pairwise signals, im-proves hallucination detection accuracy. Notably, RandPair lags on MediaSum and MeetB, consistent with the intuition that random same-/different-label pairing induces many weak positives and easy negatives that fail to enforce document-sensitive distinctions. CPIL’s superiority underscores the importance of the pairing/training strate-gies designed to hallucination detections. 

(ii) Pivot selection. We next examine single-pivot instantiations, denoted Pivot-es /zh /fr , which uses only one back-translation pivot (Spanish, Chinese, or French, respectively). Single-pivot training consistently underperforms CPIL’s multi-view setting. Among indi-vidual pivots, Pivot-zh is strongest overall, whereas Pivot-es lags markedly behind Pivot-zh and Pivot-fr .We hypothesize the contributing factor as: Pivots that differ more in morphology and word order from en (e.g., zh ) tend to induce richer syntactic rephrasings yet still return faithful en render-ings, which produce harder but valid positives. Conversely, closer pivots (e.g., es ) often yield conservative paraphrases with limited structural variety, diminishing augmentation value. 

Paraphrase diversity helps. Multi-pivot paraphrasing miti-gates pivot-specific drawbacks: combining views increases syn-tactic/lexical diversity while averaging out pivot-induced errors. Consequently, CPIL’s multi-view version improves macro F1 over any single-pivot variant, indicating that view diversity under label invariance could be the key. 

4. CONCLUSION 

We presented CPIL, a two-stage Siamese framework for hallucina-tion detection that maximizes the utility of existing labels. CPIL con-verts individual labeled examples into pairs by considering cross-paraphrase positives and same-document, opposite-label hard neg-atives . A contrastive pretraining stage learns a paraphrase invari-ant representation, followed by a lightweight classifier finetuning stage with single pass inference. On the LLM-AggreFact bench-mark, CPIL achieves state-of-the-art macro F1 with only ∼1% of labels and remains competitive at even smaller budgets. In the future, we will explore: (i) improving faithfulness of back translation with safeguards against semantic drift, for example entailment based filters; (ii) addressing the performance plateau at moderate label budgets using larger encoders (e.g., Flan-T5 [25]), adaptive hard negative mining, and greater view diversity through more paraphrase strategies; and (iii) extending CPIL to multi sen-tence claims, multi hop contexts, and multi modal grounding [26].
